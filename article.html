Florida Turnpike Expansion: What Boca Raton Owners Need to Know

By Samantha Gornstein · The Edmund Bogen Team at Douglas Elliman · September 25, 2026
This article was written with the assistance of artificial intelligence.

The Florida Turnpike widening through West Boca Raton is no longer a rumor. It is designed, funded, and scheduled: construction begins in the third quarter of 2027, and the roadway between Glades Road and the L-38 Canal grows from six lanes to eight, plus an auxiliary lane in each direction.

Every club community in the corridor is fielding the same questions right now — and most of the answers circulating are wrong. So here is the actual picture, drawn from FDOT's own documents: what is being built, when, and how the effects fall differently depending on where your home sits.

For owners closest to the right-of-way, the headline is one most have not heard: FDOT's draft analysis recommends more than a mile of new noise walls, and the best available research says that for homes nearest those walls, sound barriers have historically supported property values rather than hurt them. For owners farther east — the Polo Club among them — the story is different, and smaller, but still worth understanding.

What Exactly Is Being Built

The controlling project is FDOT Project 417132-1, covering roughly 3.4 miles of the Turnpike from north of Glades Road to north of the L-38 Canal. In West Boca, that is the stretch running between Clint Moore Road and the canal — the segment that passes directly along St. Andrews Country Club's western boundary, west of Jog Road.

The design includes four general-use lanes in each direction (up from three), one auxiliary lane in each direction, replacement of the Yamato Road and Clint Moore Road bridges, a realigned southbound Glades Road exit ramp, and new noise walls where FDOT's criteria are met. Roughly $304.5 million in construction funding is programmed for fiscal year 2027 — the highest-certainty tier in the state's work program.

Three facts matter for owners across the corridor, and all three are favorable:

No land is being taken. The widening fits within the existing Turnpike right-of-way. Programmed right-of-way acquisition for the entire 3.4-mile segment is under $250,000 — a rounding error. No club homes and no golf-course land are identified for acquisition.

No new tolls. The added lanes are general-purpose lanes, not tolled express lanes.

The noise walls are recommended, not speculative. FDOT's 2026 draft noise study analyzed 152 St. Andrews residences plus golf-course locations and recommends approximately 5,675 feet — about 1.1 miles — of barriers along that community, most of it 14 feet high, at a cost of roughly $2.69 million. The study projects an average noise reduction of about 7.5 decibels for the homes that benefit.

The Timeline

Design finishes at the end of 2026. Construction on the Clint Moore–to–canal segment begins in the third quarter of 2027 and is expected to run approximately five years — call it 2032. The segments to the north (L-38 to Atlantic Avenue, then Atlantic to Boynton Beach Boulevard) follow in fiscal 2029 and 2031, so corridor-wide work continues into the early 2030s even after the West Boca stretch is done.

If You Own in the Polo Club

Geography is doing you a favor here, and it is worth being precise about why. The Polo Club's roughly 1,100-acre footprint runs east of Jog Road, spanning about a mile and a half between Jog and Military Trail. The Turnpike right-of-way sits west of Jog — on the far side of St. Andrews — putting the Polo Club's nearest boundary well over a mile from the construction.

That distance matters because it is far outside the range where highway proximity shows up in the data at all. The hedonic-pricing literature finds noise and proximity effects fading to nearly nothing beyond about 400 meters. The Polo Club sits several times that distance away.

It also means the noise-wall story is not a Polo Club story. FDOT's draft noise study evaluates receptors adjacent to the right-of-way — St. Andrews is the West Boca club community in that analysis — and Polo Club parcels are not among the receptors identified for abatement. Owners here should not expect a wall, and should not expect a wall-driven value effect in either direction.

What Polo Club owners will actually feel is the construction period itself: detours and lane closures on Clint Moore Road, added pressure at the Glades Road interchange, truck traffic on Jog, and the ordinary friction of a five-year job a mile to the west. Real, but temporary — and a different conversation than the one perimeter owners in St. Andrews are having.

What This Means for Property Values

This is the question every owner in the corridor should be asking, and the honest answer is: it depends on where your home sits — and on the difference between the construction years and what comes after.

Interior and eastern communities — the Polo Club included — should see little direct effect. Beyond roughly 400 meters, club economics, renovation quality, and lot characteristics keep driving prices, exactly as they do today. In the Polo Club specifically, the variables that move value are the ones that already move it: membership structure, the pace of interior renovations, and lake-versus-golf positioning.

For perimeter homes along the right-of-way, the walls are the story. A September 2025 National Bureau of Economic Research working paper studied nearly 600,000 Florida properties around newly built highway sound barriers. Homes within 100 meters of a new wall saw prices rise approximately 6.8 percent — a gain that proved immediate and largely permanent — with measurable benefits extending out to 400 meters. The researchers estimated roughly a one-percent value gain per decibel of noise reduction. Apply that lens to a projected 7.5-decibel average reduction behind a 14-foot wall, and the post-completion math for Turnpike-adjacent homes starts to look like an upgrade over today's condition, not a downgrade.

We also have a local precedent. When I-95 was widened through Boca Raton and Delray Beach, the state installed a chain of sound walls along the adjacent neighborhoods. Residents described the before-and-after difference as night and day. That project is the closest real-world analog to what the Turnpike-adjacent communities will experience.

The construction window is the trade-off. From late 2027 until the walls and landscaping are complete, expect the usual drag on the most exposed listings: fewer buyers willing to look past visible construction, longer marketing times, more negotiation. Studies of comparable highway projects found values on nearby homes dipped modestly during construction and recovered once the work finished. Owners who can choose their timing should factor that arc into their plans.

The caveat worth respecting: not every perimeter home gets the full benefit. The wall sections at the north and south ends of the St. Andrews frontage are limited to 8 feet because they must sit atop existing retaining structures, and FDOT's own analysis says some homes there will fall short of the state's minimum five-decibel benefit standard — the threshold a wall must deliver for a home to count as benefited, distinct from the 7.5-decibel average projected for the homes that do benefit. The difference between an 8-foot section and a 14-foot section could matter to an individual property. That is a parcel-level question — and it is answerable.

What Smart Owners Do Now

The design is final at the end of this year. Between now and then, the advantage goes to owners who get specific:

1. Know your actual distance. Before anything else, establish where your parcel sits relative to the right-of-way. For most Polo Club owners the answer ends the conversation; for owners along the corridor's western edge, it starts one. I can pull the FDOT maps for your exact address either way.

2. Document today's baseline. Owners near the work should capture current conditions — sound, sightlines — so the before-and-after case is provable when they sell.

3. If you're selling in the next 18 months, the project is now a known, disclosable fact — Florida law requires disclosure of material facts affecting value. The right move is to get ahead of it with accurate information: the wall recommendation, no land takings, no new tolls, and the value-recovery research. Buyers fear the unknown far more than they fear a documented plan with a funded mitigation. That is doubly true in communities like the Polo Club, where the honest answer is that the project is more than a mile away.

4. If you're buying, the construction window may be the opportunity. The research says the discount, where it appears, has historically been temporary — and the wall is permanent.

The bottom line: this project is coming, it is fully funded, and the state's own analysis pairs it with the most substantial noise-mitigation investment this corridor has ever been offered. How much of that matters to you depends almost entirely on your address. The owners who win the next five years will be the ones who trade rumor for parcel-level facts.
